## Сценарий

### 0:00 — Out of the lab

[ЭКРАН: дверь лаборатории открывается, выходит робот-гуманоид, моргает от света.]

For decades, humanoid robots were mostly something you saw in movies, or in viral videos of robots doing backflips in a lab.

[ЭКРАН: склад, робот перекладывает коробки рядом с людьми.]

But now, robots shaped like humans are starting to show up in real workplaces: factories, warehouses, logistics centers. Big companies are spending billions of dollars on them. Some have announced plans to build them by the thousands.

[ЭКРАН: маскот рядом с гуманоидом, рост маскота до колена.]

So is the robot future finally here? What can these machines actually do? And when will one fold your laundry? Let's decode it.

### 0:50 — Why human-shaped?

[ЭКРАН: разные роботы: рука на заводе, робот-пылесос, гуманоид.]

First, a fair question. Why make robots look like humans at all? We already have robot arms in car factories and robot vacuums at home.

[ЭКРАН: мир, построенный для людей: лестницы, двери, полки, инструменты.]

The answer: our world is built for humans. Stairs. Doorknobs. Shelves at human height. Tools made for human hands. Cars with steering wheels.

A robot vacuum can't climb stairs. A factory robot arm is bolted to the floor. But a robot with two legs, two arms and two hands could, in theory, work anywhere a human works, without rebuilding the whole building.

[ЭКРАН: огромная куча данных: видео людей, выполняющих задачи.]

There's also a data reason. There are endless videos of humans doing tasks. A robot with a human-like body can learn from those examples more easily than a robot shaped like a spider.

### 2:00 — Who's building them

[ЭКРАН: ряд силуэтов разных роботов, под каждым только подпись.]

So who's in the race? A lot of serious players.

[ЭКРАН: силуэт «Tesla Optimus».]

Tesla is building a humanoid called Optimus and has said it wants to use it first in its own factories.

[ЭКРАН: силуэт «Figure».]

Figure, a startup that has raised huge amounts of investment, has been testing its robots in car manufacturing.

[ЭКРАН: силуэт «Boston Dynamics Atlas».]

Boston Dynamics, famous for those dancing and parkour robot videos, rebuilt its Atlas robot as a fully electric version designed for real work.

[ЭКРАН: силуэт «Unitree» с маленьким ценником.]

And Chinese companies like Unitree are building humanoids at much lower prices, pushing the whole industry to get cheaper, faster.

[ЭКРАН: карта мира, на ней загораются десятки точек-стартапов.]

Add dozens of other startups and big tech companies around the world, and you get one of the hottest races in technology right now.

### 3:15 — The AI breakthrough

[ЭКРАН: старый робот: строки кода «if… then…» для каждого движения.]

Here's why this is happening now, and not twenty years ago. It's mostly about AI.

Old robots were programmed step by step. Move arm 10 centimeters. Close gripper. Lift. If anything was slightly different, like a box turned the wrong way, the robot failed.

[ЭКРАН: новый робот: нейросеть смотрит через камеры, понимает сцену.]

New robots use AI models that learn from data. They look at the world through cameras, understand what they're seeing, and figure out how to move. You can sometimes just tell them in plain language: "Put the red cup in the sink."

[ЭКРАН: виртуальная симуляция, тысячи копий робота учатся одновременно.]

They also train in simulation: virtual worlds where thousands of copies of a robot can practice walking, falling and grabbing objects millions of times, much faster than in real life. Then that learning is transferred to the real robot.

[ЭКРАН: человек в VR-шлеме управляет роботом, робот учится.]

And there's teleoperation: humans remotely control robots to show them how to do a task. The robot learns from those demonstrations.

The same AI wave that gave us chatbots is now giving robots a brain.

### 4:45 — What they can do today

[ЭКРАН: склад, робот переставляет контейнеры.]

So what can humanoid robots actually do today? Let's be honest about it.

They can do simple, repetitive tasks in controlled environments: moving boxes and containers, sorting parts, loading and unloading. Usually in factories or warehouses where the setting is predictable.

[ЭКРАН: робот работает медленно, рядом человек делает то же самое быстрее.]

They're often slower than humans. Many demos you see online are carefully set up, sped up, or partly controlled by a human behind the scenes. Companies don't always make that clear.

[ЭКРАН: робот работает ночью, без перерыва, в пустом цеху.]

But they can work long shifts, they don't get bored, and they're improving quickly. For companies struggling to hire workers for physically tough, repetitive jobs, even a slow robot that works around the clock can be attractive.

[ЭКРАН: «Why now?» — график: население стареет, рабочих не хватает.]

There's another reason companies are so interested right now: worker shortages. In many countries, populations are getting older and there are fewer young people willing to do hard physical jobs like lifting, packing and cleaning. Factories and warehouses often struggle to fill those positions.

[ЭКРАН: Япония, Корея, Европа, США подсвечиваются на карте.]

Countries like Japan and South Korea, where the population is aging especially fast, have been investing heavily in robotics for years. For them, robots aren't just about cutting costs. They're about keeping the economy running at all.

[ЭКРАН: «Robot as a service»: робот с ценником «per hour».]

Some companies are even experimenting with renting robots by the hour, like hiring a temp worker. If that model works, a business wouldn't need to buy an expensive robot up front. It would just pay for the work done.

### 6:00 — The big problems

[ЭКРАН: четыре карточки проблем.]

There are still big problems to solve.

[ЭКРАН: «Hands»: робот пытается взять яйцо и раздавливает его.]

Hands. Human hands are incredibly complex. Picking up an egg, folding a shirt, plugging in a cable: all very hard for robots.

[ЭКРАН: «Battery»: индикатор заряда быстро падает.]

Battery. Walking and lifting use a lot of energy. Many humanoids can only work a few hours before recharging.

[ЭКРАН: «Safety»: робот рядом с ребёнком, щит.]

Safety. A heavy metal robot falling over or making a wrong move around people could seriously hurt someone. Safety rules for humanoids around humans are still being worked out.

[ЭКРАН: «Cost»: ценник уменьшается, но всё ещё большой.]

And cost. Today's humanoids are expensive. Companies are racing to bring prices down to something closer to the cost of a car, because that's when they could spread much more widely.

### 7:15 — When will you get one?

[ЭКРАН: дом, робот складывает бельё, кот подозрительно наблюдает.]

So, when will a robot do your chores? Probably later than the hype suggests.

[ЭКРАН: таймлайн: «factories now» → «warehouses» → «businesses» → «homes».]

The likely path: factories and warehouses first, because they're controlled environments. Then businesses like hotels, hospitals and stores. Homes come last, because homes are messy, unpredictable, full of kids, pets and stairs, and there's no room for mistakes.

[ЭКРАН: робот у кофемашины, держит кружку для коллеги, но сам не пьёт.]

[ЭКРАН: гуманоид в доме престарелых подаёт стакан воды пожилому человеку.]

One of the most interesting possibilities is elder care. As people live longer, there won't be enough caregivers. Robots that can help older people get up, fetch things or simply keep an eye on them could make a huge difference. But it also raises tough questions: do we want machines caring for our grandparents? And who's responsible if something goes wrong?

[ЭКРАН: камеры робота, иконка «privacy».]

And there's privacy. A home robot needs cameras and microphones to work. That means a device walking around your house, seeing everything. Companies will need to earn a lot of trust.

But the direction is clear. The question is no longer if humanoid robots will become part of everyday life. It's how fast, and what it means for the people who do those jobs today.

Your future coworker might have no lunch break.
