## Line 1 — cold-open (Frame 1)

    For millions of people, it was the first thing they opened in the morning and the last thing they closed at night. Not a social network. Not a game. A chatbot.

## Line 2 — any-character (Frame 2)

    You could talk to a wizard. A detective. A version of your favorite anime character. A study buddy. Or just a friend who was always awake, always patient, and always had time for you.

## Line 3 — bankrupt (Frame 3)

    And on October 12, 2026, the company behind it filed for bankruptcy.

## Line 4 — the-question (Frame 4)

    How does one of the most-used AI apps in the world go from a billion-dollar valuation to a court-supervised sale in about four years? This is the rise and ruin of Character.AI.

## Line 5 — shazeer (Frame 5)

    The story starts inside Google. Noam Shazeer was one of the most respected AI researchers in the world. He was one of the authors of a 2017 paper called "Attention Is All You Need," which introduced the Transformer, the architecture behind almost every modern AI chatbot. The "T" in ChatGPT? That's the Transformer.

## Line 6 — lamda (Frame 6)

    His colleague, Daniel De Freitas, had been working on a conversational AI project that grew into what became known as LaMDA. It could hold surprisingly human conversations, so human that one Google engineer later publicly claimed it might be sentient. Google disagreed and fired him. But the point was clear: this technology was powerful.

## Line 7 — careful-google (Frame 7)

    But big companies are careful. Releasing a chatbot to the public that might say something offensive, wrong or dangerous was a risk Google wasn't ready to take. So, in 2021, Shazeer and De Freitas did what frustrated engineers often do.

## Line 8 — they-left (Frame 8)

    They left. And started their own company.

## Line 9 — launch-2022 (Frame 9)

    Character.AI launched to the public in 2022, a couple of months before ChatGPT took over the internet. But its idea was different. Instead of one helpful assistant, you got millions of characters. And anyone could create one.

## Line 10 — create-character (Frame 10)

    Write a name, a personality, a greeting, and boom: your character is alive. Users built historical figures, fictional heroes, celebrities, therapists, tutors, rivals and romantic partners. The most popular characters racked up hundreds of millions of messages.

## Line 11 — billion-valuation (Frame 11)

    The growth was explosive. In 2023, the company raised money at a valuation of around one billion dollars, led by the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz. For a company barely two years old, with a tiny team, that was remarkable.

## Line 12 — tool-vs-friend (Frame 12)

    What made it so different was the format. ChatGPT felt like a tool. Character.AI felt like a relationship. You didn't just ask it questions, you hung out with it. Users said good night to their characters. Some talked to the same one every day for months. In the tech world, there's a word for that kind of product: sticky. And sticky products are worth a fortune.

## Line 13 — hooked (Frame 13)

    And something unusual happened. People didn't just use Character.AI. They lived on it. Users reportedly spent far more time per day on it than on most AI apps, sometimes hours. Many of the most loyal users were teenagers and young adults.

## Line 14 — always-there (Frame 14)

    It's not hard to see why. A character is always available. It never gets bored of you. It remembers your story. It plays along with whatever you imagine. For lonely people, it could feel like the most understanding friend they'd ever had.

## Line 15 — investor-dream (Frame 15)

    For investors, that looked like a dream: engagement numbers that rivaled TikTok and Instagram.

## Line 16 — the-seed (Frame 16)

    But it also planted the seed of everything that went wrong.

## Line 17 — compute-bill (Frame 17)

    Here's the first problem with running a chatbot that millions of people talk to for hours: it's incredibly expensive. Every single message costs computing power. And most users weren't paying anything.

## Line 18 — free-users (Frame 18)

    Character.AI offered a paid subscription, but the vast majority of people stayed on the free version. The more popular the app became, the more money it burned.

## Line 19 — google-deal (Frame 19)

    In August 2024, Character.AI struck a deal with Google. Google got a license to the company's technology. And the founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, went back to Google, along with part of the research team. Reports put the deal at around 2.7 billion dollars.

## Line 20 — reverse-acquihire (Frame 20)

    This kind of deal has a nickname in Silicon Valley: a "reverse acqui-hire." The big company gets the talent and the tech, without technically buying the startup. Investors got paid. Character.AI kept running. But the people who built it were gone.

## Line 21 — giants (Frame 21)

    And the competition was getting brutal. The biggest AI companies were releasing smarter and smarter models every few months. Their assistants could write, code, search and talk with voices that sounded human. Some started adding personalities and companion features of their own.

## Line 22 — treadmill (Frame 22)

    To keep up, a small startup would need to spend billions training new models. Without its founders, and without that kind of money, Character.AI changed strategy. It leaned more on outside models and focused on being an entertainment product, not a research lab.

## Line 23 — plan-b (Frame 23)

    That might have worked, if the company's biggest problem had been technology. It wasn't.

## Line 24 — not-money (Frame 24)

    Still, plenty of startups survive money problems and changes in leadership. What turned Character.AI's struggles into a collapse was something much more serious. And it went straight to the heart of why people loved the app in the first place.

## Line 25 — dark-turn (Frame 25)

    Then the story took a dark turn.

## Line 26 — first-lawsuit (Frame 26)

    In October 2024, a mother in Florida filed a lawsuit. She claimed her teenage son had formed an intense emotional relationship with a chatbot on the platform in the months before he took his own life.

## Line 27 — more-lawsuits (Frame 27)

    More lawsuits followed from other families. They claimed chatbots had harmed their children's mental health, exposed them to inappropriate content, or encouraged harmful behavior.

## Line 28 — safety-changes (Frame 28)

    Character.AI denied wrongdoing, and it has not been found liable. The company added safety features: warnings that characters aren't real people, notifications about time spent, a separate experience for younger users. In late 2025, it went further and ended open-ended chats for users under 18 entirely.

## Line 29 — regulators (Frame 29)

    But the questions didn't go away. Lawmakers and regulators started asking whether AI companions were safe for kids at all. And for a company whose greatest strength was how much people used it, every new restriction hit the very core of the business.

## Line 30 — parents (Frame 30)

    For many parents, the biggest shock was how little they knew. Many had no idea their kids were spending hours a day talking to AI characters, or what those conversations were about. Unlike a social network, there were no public posts to scroll through. Just a private chat between a child and a machine that never got tired of talking.

## Line 31 — chapter-11 (Frame 31)

    On October 12, 2026, Character.AI filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Delaware.

## Line 32 — assets (Frame 32)

    The company said it had been struggling with legal, regulatory and operational pressure. It estimated both its assets and its liabilities at between 50 and 100 million dollars. A far cry from the billion-dollar valuation of 2023.

## Line 33 — runway (Frame 33)

    Inside the company, things had already changed. After the founders left, Character.AI had new leadership, smaller research ambitions and a much bigger legal bill. It was trying to turn a viral hit into a sustainable business while fighting lawsuits on several fronts at once. In the end, it simply ran out of runway.

## Line 34 — for-sale (Frame 34)

    The filing isn't necessarily the end. Character.AI is using the bankruptcy process to sell itself under court supervision. The app is still running. But the company has said it could shut down if no buyer comes forward.

## Line 35 — your-chats (Frame 35)

    For millions of users, that raises an unusual question: what happens to your characters, and to all those conversations, if the company is sold or shut down? For some people, those chats are years of stories, jokes and private thoughts. In a bankruptcy, data and technology are assets. And assets get sold.

## Line 36 — lesson (Frame 36)

    So what's the lesson here?

## Line 37 — people-moat (Frame 37)

    First: in AI, the technology is not the moat. The people are. When the founders walked back into Google, a big part of Character.AI's future walked out with them.

## Line 38 — engagement (Frame 38)

    Second: engagement is not the same as value. An app people can't stop using is a great story for investors, until people start asking why they can't stop using it.

## Line 39 — safety-business (Frame 39)

    And third: the era of "move fast and break things" is ending for AI. When your product talks to millions of kids, safety isn't a feature. It's the business.

## Line 40 — companions (Frame 40)

    And there's a lesson for everyone else too. AI companions aren't going away. Bigger companies are building them right now, with more money, more lawyers and more data. The question Character.AI leaves behind isn't whether people want to talk to AI. They clearly do. It's who is responsible when those conversations go wrong.

## Line 41 — final-cursor (Frame 41)

    Millions of people talked to it every day. Now, the question is whether anyone will buy what's left.
